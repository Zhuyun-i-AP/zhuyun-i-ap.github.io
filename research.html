Systematic Signed Jump Variation and Chinese Aggregate Stock Returns

Zhuyun Jiang, Jun Tu, and Lei Zhang.

Working Paper.

Abstract: Using 5-minute intraday data for a large cross section of individual stocks, we construct a relative signed jump (RSJ) variation measure and partition it into systematic-jump-day (RSJS) and non-systematic-jump-day (RSJN) components. We find that while aggregate RSJ predicts market returns with positive coefficients, the predictive power is primarily driven by the systematic-jump-day component (RSJS). The relation remains significant in-sample after controlling individually for economic, realized, and liquidity variables, improves forecasts out-of-sample, and generates economically meaningful asset-allocation gains. Further analyses provide evidence consistent with the interpretation that RSJS captures market-wide information about future fundamentals that is gradually incorporated into prices. Specifically, its return predictability is primarily associated with VAR-estimated cash-flow news. Higher RSJS also predicts more favorable subsequent market-wide financial-news tone, stronger corporate profitability, and improved macroeconomic activity.

Keywords: Relative signed jump variation, Systematic jump, Return predictability, Chinese stock index returns.

Diverging Tails: Stock-Level Tail Fears and Return Reversal

Zhuyun Jiang and Xiangwei Wan.

Working Paper.

Abstract: We introduce the Firm-Specific Tail Insurance Price (FTIP), the price of a firm-specific extreme downside-insurance portfolio for an individual stock. We find that stocks with high FTIP earn significantly higher returns in the cross-section. A high-minus-low FTIP portfolio earns 1.35% (1.05%) per month on an equal-weighted (value-weighted) basis. We interpret this finding through a pessimistic-beliefs hypothesis: a pessimistic-belief component embedded in FTIP depresses stock valuations, generating higher subsequent returns when the underlying pessimism extends beyond what firm fundamentals justify. Consistent with this hypothesis, high-FTIP firms are associated with more pessimistic beliefs and greater shorting demand, followed by an easing of bearish positioning, favorable earnings surprises, and higher subsequent stock returns.

Keywords: Tail Insurance, Put Options, Investor Belief, Return Predictability.

Strategic Climate Innovation and Supply Chain Power

Zhuyun Jiang, Buhui Qiu, Hao Tian, and Yi Zhang.

Working Paper.

Abstract: This paper examines whether climate innovation is associated with firms' bargaining positions within supply chains. Using Chinese A-share listed firms from 2006 to 2024, we measure climate innovation using cumulative climate-related patents and proxy for buyer power using the negative of the purchase share attributable to firms' five largest suppliers. We find that firms with greater climate innovation exhibit lower dependence on major suppliers. This association remains robust after controlling for non-climate patenting and across alternative specifications. Complementary evidence from a propensity-score-matched pair-stacked difference-in-differences design and a policy-exposure instrumental-variable approach supports the baseline findings. Additional analyses show that climate innovation is associated with greater market-implied climate resilience, more favorable external assessments of stakeholder-related performance, and stronger operating outcomes. The association is stronger among firms exposed to carbon trading and greater product-market competition, but weaker in pollution-intensive industries and regions with greater environmental policy attention. Overall, our findings identify supplier dependence as an economically relevant dimension of climate innovation and highlight the potential role of climate-related technological capabilities in shaping firms' supply-chain positions.

Keywords: Climate Innovation; Supply Chain Dependence; Buyer Power; Climate Risk; Supplier Relationships.
